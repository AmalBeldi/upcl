\section{In one paragraph}

An agent rarely observes the state of its environment directly: it infers it from noisy sensors or predictive models. Most pipelines nevertheless collapse that inference to a single most-likely state \emph{before} acting, which discards exactly the information a risk-sensitive decision rule needs. UPCL keeps the uncertainty. It represents the uncertain state as a probability distribution over \emph{possible worlds}, scores every candidate action in every world, and aggregates the world-wise scores with a pluggable risk criterion (Expected Utility, Maxmin, Hurwicz, CVaR, DRO) before selecting an action. The belief is built independently of the policy that consumes it, so an existing recommender or controller can be made uncertainty-aware without being rewritten. This repository implements the framework, checks every formal statement of the paper with a unit test, and reproduces every reported number from a script and a frozen log.

\section{The approach}

\paragraph{1. Belief construction.} A \emph{probabilistic context instance} $\kappa_u^t=\{P(C_1),\dots,P(C_K)\}$ gives, for each discrete state variable $C_k$ (mood, location, \dots), a distribution over its values. The transformation $\Phi$ turns it into a probabilistic context space $(\Omega,\mathbb P)$: $\Omega$ is the set of complete assignments (possible worlds) and $\mathbb P$ their probabilities, under tuple-independent (TID), block-independent-disjoint (BID) or hybrid semantics borrowed from probabilistic databases.

\paragraph{2. Aggregation and decision.} For a user $u$ and item (action) $i$, a policy produces a world-wise score $r(u,i,\omega)$. A generic operator $\Gamma_\theta$ maps $\{(r(u,i,\omega),\mathbb P(\omega))\}_\omega$ to one score $S_\theta(u,i)$; the decision operator $\Psi=\arg\max_i S_\theta(u,i)$ then picks the action. Changing the risk attitude changes only $\Gamma_\theta$; $\Phi$ and $\Psi$ are untouched.

\paragraph{3. Three integration points.} The same belief can meet the policy at three places, so one belief layer serves policies of different kinds:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{UPCL-Pre}: evaluate the policy under every world, then aggregate (needs a policy callable per world).
\item \textbf{UPCL-Model}: encode the belief into a fixed-size vector $z$ and feed it to a learned policy $f_\varphi(u,i,z)$ (one call, needs training).
\item \textbf{UPCL-Post}: call a black-box base policy $f_0(u,i)$ once, adjust its score per world with $h_\eta$, then aggregate (works for policies that cannot be retrained).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{4. Scale.} Enumerating $\Omega$ costs $\prod_k m_k$. A Monte Carlo estimator samples $N$ worlds and carries a Hoeffding finite-sample bound, with a closed-form rule $N\ge\frac{(b-a)^2}{2\varepsilon^2}\ln\frac{2}{\delta}$ for a target accuracy.

\paragraph{A ten-second illustration.} Alice's mood is Happy with probability $0.65$, Tired with $0.35$. Item A scores $4.8$/$3.2$ in the two worlds, item B scores $3.9$/$4.5$. Expected Utility gives $4.24$ for A and $4.11$ for B, so A is chosen; Maxmin gives $3.2$ and $3.9$, so B is chosen. The belief, the world-wise scores and $\Psi$ are identical; only $\Gamma_\theta$ changed. (\code{experiments/example\_alice.py} reproduces this.)

\section{What it adds relative to existing work}

The paper positions UPCL against four bodies of work, none of which covers all of the following three requirements at once: (i)~a declarative, policy-agnostic belief over the agent's state; (ii)~a decision layer linking that belief to actions under both risk-neutral and risk-sensitive criteria; (iii)~independence from the policy that consumes the belief.

\begin{center}
\small

\begin{tabular}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{3.6cm}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{5.3cm}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{5.6cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Line of work} & \textbf{What it provides} & \textbf{What is missing for the setting above}\\
\midrule
Belief-state planning (POMDPs) & A principled belief over hidden state & Belief and policy are coupled in one solver; no pluggable risk criterion over an \emph{existing} policy\\
Risk-sensitive decision theory (Maxmin, Hurwicz, CVaR, DRO) & Criteria for acting under a distribution or ambiguity set & Assumes a belief is already available; says nothing about constructing it\\
Probabilistic databases (TID/BID, possible worlds) & Semantics for uncertain data & No decision layer; no notion of acting on the result\\
Context-aware recommenders (pre-/post-filtering, contextual models) & Use of situational context & Context treated as exact or absent; uncertainty collapsed before acting\\
Bayesian / conformal uncertainty for recommenders & Calibrated predictive uncertainty on scores & Uncertainty on the \emph{prediction}, not on the observed \emph{state}; one point-estimate rule at a time\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent The contribution is therefore a \emph{composition}: probabilistic-database semantics to represent the state, a generic risk operator to decide, and three integration points to attach the whole to any policy, together with (a)~consistency and reduction guarantees (a degenerate belief recovers the deterministic decision exactly), (b)~Monte Carlo convergence and finite-sample bounds, and (c)~an impossibility result showing that handing the policy a single realisation cannot preserve uncertainty while remaining policy-independent. The evaluation uses context-aware recommendation because its ground truth is tractable; nothing in the framework is specific to it.

\paragraph{Minimal usage.}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
from upcl.context import ContextualVariable, ProbabilisticContextInstance, phi
from upcl.instantiations import upcl_pre

mood = ContextualVariable("Mood", {"Happy": 0.65, "Tired": 0.35})
space = phi(ProbabilisticContextInstance(categorical_variables=[mood]))

# any callable f(user, item, world) -> score; policy="eu" | "maxmin" |
# "hurwicz" | "cvar" | "dro" (extra kwargs, e.g. alpha=, beta=, are forwarded)
best, scores = upcl_pre("Alice", ["A", "B"], space, my_policy, policy="maxmin")
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Design choices worth knowing.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{One call signature for all three strategies and all five operators}, so swapping a risk criterion or an integration point is a one-argument change.
\item \emph{Worlds are tuples of \code{(variable, value)} pairs}, so a policy can read only the variables it cares about; zero-probability worlds are pruned by default.
\item \emph{UPCL-Post treats the base policy as a black box} (\code{base\_recommender(u,i)}); only the adjustment $h_\eta$ and $\Gamma_\theta$ see the belief.
\item \emph{Everything stochastic takes an explicit seed}; the experiment scripts are deterministic, and the committed logs are their exact output.
\end{itemize}

\section{Checking and reproducing the claims}

\noindent The synthetic scripts need no data. The real-data scripts need the raw benchmark from its official source (no dataset is redistributed); \code{prepare\_real\_dataset.py} converts it to the tidy schema, and \code{README.md} lists the exact commands.

\paragraph{Evaluation protocol.} Robustness is measured by moving each state variable toward the uniform distribution with intensity $\lambda\in\{0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1\}$ and comparing against a baseline that collapses the (noisy) belief to its mode. Every comparison is a paired two-sided $t$-test with paired Cohen's $d$; where a claim rests on several tests, Holm--Bonferroni correction is applied across the whole family.

\section{Scope and anonymity}

The framework assumes discrete state variables and, for the closed-form TID/BID constructions, independence between blocks (a joint model can replace the probability-assignment step). The repository contains no author names, affiliations or identifying history, and it redistributes no third-party dataset; it is meant to be browsed through an anonymised mirror during review.
